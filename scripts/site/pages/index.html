Take your tabs with you.

TabsPack packs every open tab into one small file, and puts them back exactly as they were: the same windows, the same order, the same pinned tabs, the same groups. In the same browser, or a different one.

Why anyone needs this

A browser is a terrible place to keep something important.

Everything you are in the middle of lives in a row of tabs that any one of these can take away.

You have too many to close

Ninety tabs, and every one of them is the only record that you were going to read it. Closing the window costs something real, so the window never closes, and the machine pays for it all day.

You are moving browsers

A new laptop, a work profile, Chrome to Firefox. Bookmarks come across and the session does not: the windows, the order and the pinned tabs, which is the part you actually had in your head.

Something took them away

A crash, an update, an extension you installed that was later delisted and left every tab parked behind its own page. Restore-last-session is one click deep and one chance wide.

What it does

Everything comes back, not just the addresses.

Most exporters give you a list of links. A list of links is not a session. TabsPack keeps the shape.

Windows, order and pinned tabs

Three windows come back as three windows, in the order you had them, with the pinned tabs pinned and the active tab active. Position and size too.

Tab groups, with their names

Titles, colours and whether a group was collapsed. TabsPack asks for the tab groups permission before you export, because that is where a group's name is lost if it does not.

Two hundred tabs, asleep

Restored tabs open unloaded: title and address in the strip, nothing running behind them. A pack of 200 real pages restores in about nine seconds and leaves 199 of them asleep.

It unwraps suspended tabs

Suspenders park your tab on their own page and hide the real address inside it, which no other browser can reopen. TabsPack reads it back out, on export and on import, and counts them for you.

Snapshots, without a file

Name the session and keep it inside the extension for the days you do not want to think about where a file goes. Snapshots survive a restart, and they are still only on this machine.

Keyboard, not menus

Alt + Shift + E exports everything, Alt + Shift + D this window, Alt + Shift + S saves a snapshot. The toolbar icon tells you it worked.

How it works

Three clicks out, three clicks back.

  1. Pack them

    Open TabsPack, choose this window or all of them, and press Export. You get one .tabspack.json file, named with the date, in your downloads folder.

  2. Take it anywhere

    Email it, drop it in a repository, put it on a stick. It is a small text file and it has no secrets in it: no cookies, no tokens, nothing that could sign in as you.

  3. Unpack them

    In any browser with TabsPack, drop the file in. You see exactly what is about to open before anything opens, then press Restore and your session is back.

Privacy

Your tabs never leave this device.

Not as a policy. As a property of the build: TabsPack asks for no host permission when you install it, so out of the box it cannot reach any address on the internet at all.

  • No account and no sync. There is nothing to sign in to, so there is nothing of yours anywhere to sign in to.
  • No analytics, no telemetry, no crash reporting. Nothing counts what you do, because nothing is watching.
  • No page access. No content script is injected anywhere, so TabsPack cannot read or change a page even if it wanted to.
  • An export cannot log in as you. A pack holds addresses, titles and structure. Never cookies, tokens, headers or form data. That is written into the format specification, not left to the implementation.
  • One request exists, and you make it. Pressing Send in the Support pane sends the message you wrote. Your browser asks first, declining is fine, and nothing about your tabs is in it.

How the claim is kept honest.

The build refuses to compile if anyone adds a network call. scripts/lint.mjs fails on fetch, XMLHttpRequest, EventSource, WebSocket or importScripts anywhere in the source, with one named exception: the eighty lines that send a support message and nothing else.

It also fails on any remote script, font, stylesheet or image. Everything TabsPack loads comes out of the package you installed.

The file

A format you can read without us.

A .tabspack.json file is ordinary JSON against a published schema. Open it in a text editor. Diff two of them. Keep one in a repository next to the project it belongs to. Write a script that generates one.

The point is that your session is not locked inside an extension that might be delisted next year. That has happened to a lot of people, and it is most of the reason this exists.

TabsPack also exports a plain list of addresses, a flat JSON file and a CSV when that is what the next tool needs, and it tells you in the interface exactly what each of those loses.

Questions

The ones people actually ask.

Does TabsPack send my tabs anywhere?

No. It asks for no host permission when you install it, so it cannot reach any address on the internet. Your tabs are read only when you ask for an export, and they are written to a file on your own machine. The one request TabsPack can ever make is a support message you write yourself and press Send on, and your browser asks your permission before that happens.

Can I move my tabs from Chrome to Firefox?

Yes, and back again. Windows, tab order and pinned tabs come back everywhere. Tab groups come back in the browsers that have them; where they do not exist the tabs are restored without the grouping, and TabsPack says so rather than quietly dropping something.

Will restoring 200 tabs bring my computer to its knees?

No. Restored tabs open asleep: they are in the strip with their title and address, and they load nothing until you click one. A measured restore of 200 real pages takes about nine seconds and leaves 199 of them unloaded. You can turn that off if you would rather they all load.

What about tabs held by a suspender extension?

TabsPack unwraps them. Suspenders park a tab on their own page and hide the true address inside it, which is useless anywhere else and worse than useless if the suspender is ever delisted. TabsPack reads the real address back out, on export and on import, and tells you how many it found.

Is it really free? What is the catch?

It is free, there is no paid tier, there is no account, and there is nothing to sell because nothing is collected. It is published under the MIT licence and the source is on GitHub. See pricing for the longer answer, including why it is free.

Can I read the file without the extension?

Yes. It is JSON with a published schema. A text editor opens it, a script can read it, and git diff tells you what changed between two of them.

Does it work with private or incognito windows?

Private windows are left out of exports by default, and TabsPack cannot see them at all unless you explicitly allow it in private mode in your browser's own settings and switch the option on inside TabsPack. Two deliberate steps, because the default should be the careful one.

What happens to a tab I cannot reopen, like a settings page?

Browsers refuse to let an extension open their own internal pages. TabsPack keeps those addresses in the file so nothing is lost, and on restore it opens a page listing the ones it was not allowed to open, so you can click them yourself.
